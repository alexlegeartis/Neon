Convergence bounds that distinguish Fanions:
rank, step budget, curvature, and noise

Research attempt for the Neon repository

21 October 2026

Abstract

Equal exponents in worst-case convergence bounds do not imply equal finite iteration performance. We derive two loss lower bounds for exactly the quadratic class used in the manuscript: one for the rank of the accumulated update, and a stronger one for its accumulated nuclear norm. The latter gives a necessary iteration count proportional to 1/k1/k at the same learning rate, independently of momentum. On a fresh 500×500500\times500 draw from the specified distribution, Neon requires at least 65,172 iterations at learning rate 0.007 to reach loss 0.001. This is an analytic lower bound, not a training run. We also give convergence upper bounds retaining the singular spectrum, curvature, and oracle error, and quantify the robustness of F-Muon and S-Muon. A saved-log audit supports the Muon–Neon spectral comparison but also falsifies the sufficiency of a Frobenius-only explanation for all methods. These results explain a concrete mechanism behind Neon’s poor performance; they do not establish the complete CIFAR/LLM accuracy ordering.

What is established, and what is still missing

Continuation across all ranks.

The companion fanion_followup.pdf extends this first attempt with a unified plain/F/S-Fanion convergence bound, tracking for the actual code’s Nesterov convention, stronger singular-budget obstructions, solvable rank flows, and a saved quadratic trajectory audit. It also identifies a different learning-rate schedule for F-Fanion-10 in the current benchmark source. The scalar nuclear lower bounds below remain valid; the follow-up retains additional operator and spectral information to strengthen them.

The strongest result for the manuscript.

Theorem 1 applies to its synthetic objective without assuming gradient accuracy, particular momentum coefficients, or an oracle trajectory. It bounds loss, and gives a large obstruction for Neon at the learning rates actually used. A separate example gives a tight r/kr/k iteration separation between Fanion-kk and Muon even with arbitrary learning rates.

A conditional explanation for deep learning.

The appropriate comparison is the true gradient’s projection onto the update, squared, divided by curvature along that update. The singular spectrum already distinguishes Muon from Neon in most saved CIFAR snapshots. Momentum error, directional curvature, and the effect of weight normalization have not been recorded in those snapshots. Their values are needed for a complete explanation of the actual algorithms. In particular, our Frobenius surrogate incorrectly favors F-Neon over Muon; we report this failure rather than interpreting every bound as evidence for the desired ordering.

The experimental premise needs qualification.

Section 5.1 of jota_submission/main.tex reports F-Muon and S-Muon reaching the synthetic loss threshold in 910 and 890 iterations, versus 1060 for Muon. Their real-task performance is close to Muon’s. Thus the target is Muon being much better than the low-rank Fanions and comparable to its full-rank hybrids, with possible reversals among the latter.

Status of originality.

The ingredients are classical: the descent lemma, best rank approximation, norm inequalities, and gradient-estimator tracking. The Muon specialization of the descent calculation is already present in the literature. The contribution of this attempt is their explicit specialization to Fanions, the finite learning-rate obstruction for the manuscript’s quadratic, the hybrid comparison, and the audit. No claim of priority or a complete literature survey is made.

Literature check as of 21 October 2026

The statement that no relevant theory has improved is too strong.

  • Kovalev and Borodich, Non-Euclidean SGD for Structured Optimization, v2, 22 June 2026 , obtain improved guarantees under structured Hessian and gradient-noise assumptions. Their Assumption 5 puts the relevant bounding operators in a common subspace with an order-preserving projection and algebraic closure properties. Theorems 1–3 cover momentum, extrapolation, and momentum variance reduction. These are conditional structural improvements; they do not assert a universal ordering of Muon, Neon, and all hybrids. This paper is already in the local bibliography.

  • Shen et al., On the Convergence Analysis of Muon, v3, 28 July 2026 , retain matrix smoothness and Hessian information. Their proofs explicitly contain the Muon decrease ‖G‖*2/(2rL)\lVert G\rVert_*^2/(2rL) and a spectral-smoothness alternative. A spectrum-dependent Muon comparison is therefore not new by itself. Our use of all kk, hybrid directions, and a common Frobenius stopping metric makes the comparison more direct for this manuscript.

  • Nagashima and Iiduka, v3, 5 March 2026 , claim sharper iteration rates, including schedules with increasing batches. However, Corollary 3.1 defines D1=Δ/η+C3/(1−β)D_1=\Delta/\eta+C_3/(1-\beta): its D1/TD_1/T term cannot be treated as uniform when η\eta depends on TT. The displayed assertion with η=1/T\eta=1/T is contradicted by noiseless scalar f(x)=x2/2f(x)=x^2/2, x0=2x_0=2, β=0\beta=0: normalized steps of size 1/T1/T leave xt≥1x_t\geq1 through TT steps. The script reproduces this check. We do not rely on those claimed faster rates. Increasing batch size also changes oracle cost.

  • Ma et al., Schattor, June 2026 , study a Schatten family and state dimension-free stationarity guarantees in their abstract. This is further relevant work after the manuscript’s main 2025 references; an abstract claim alone is not a proof of the requested optimizer ordering.

  • Kovalev’s trust-region framework  and Gluon  remain relevant foundations. General norm guarantees must retain their geometry and use the same accuracy metric before numerical rates are compared.

In particular, improved constants under structure, faster rates under extra regularity, and a change from iteration count to total gradient evaluations are different assertions. None implies that one norm is best on every smooth objective.

A loss obstruction for exactly the manuscript’s quadratic

Consider, including a possible shift ZZ,

f(X)=12‖M1/2(X−Z)N1/2‖F2,M,N≽0,L=‖M‖op‖N‖op.\label{eq:quadratic} f(X)=\frac12\lVert M^{1/2}(X-Z)N^{1/2}\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2,\quad M,N\succeq0, \qquad L=\lVert M\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\lVert N\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}.(3)
The manuscript uses Z=0Z=0. Define E0=M1/2(X0−Z)N1/2E_0=M^{1/2}(X_0-Z)N^{1/2} and sj=σj(E0)s_j=\sigma_j(E_0). No inverse of MM or NN is required.

Theorem 1 (Rank and nuclear-norm obstructions). Let Xt+1=Xt−γtAtX_{t+1}=X_t-\gamma_t A_t, where rank⁡At≤k\operatorname{rank}A_t\leq k and ‖At‖*≤k\lVert A_t\rVert_*\leq k, with γt≥0\gamma_t\geq0. Then, for every TT,

f(XT)≥12∑j>kTsj2,f(XT)≥12r[‖E0‖*−Lk∑t<Tγt]+2.\begin{aligned} f(X_T)&\geq \frac12\sum_{j>kT}s_j^2, \label{eq:rank-bound}\\ f(X_T)&\geq\frac{1}{2r} [\lVert E_0\rVert_*-\sqrt L\,k\sum_{t<T}\gamma_t]_+^{\!2}. \label{eq:nuclear-bound} \end{aligned}(4), (5)
The rank bound only needs the rank assumption. The nuclear bound only needs the nuclear-norm assumption.

Proof. Write ET=E0−RTE_T=E_0-R_T, where RT=∑t<TγtM1/2AtN1/2R_T=\sum_{t<T}\gamma_t M^{1/2}A_tN^{1/2}. Rank subadditivity gives rank⁡RT≤kT\operatorname{rank}R_T\leq kT. The best approximation of E0E_0 by a matrix of rank at most kTkT in Frobenius norm has squared error ∑j>kTsj2\sum_{j>kT}s_j^2, proving (4). This follows directly by truncating the SVD and its standard optimality characterization.

Also, ‖M1/2AtN1/2‖*≤L‖At‖*≤Lk\lVert M^{1/2}A_tN^{1/2}\rVert_*\leq\sqrt L\lVert A_t\rVert_*\leq\sqrt Lk. The triangle inequality implies ‖ET‖*≥[‖E0‖*−Lk∑t<Tγt]+\lVert E_T\rVert_*\geq[\lVert E_0\rVert_*-\sqrt L k\sum_{t<T}\gamma_t]_+. Finally, ‖ET‖*≤r‖ET‖F\lVert E_T\rVert_*\leq\sqrt r\lVert E_T\rVert_{\mathrm F} and f(XT)=‖ET‖F2/2f(X_T)=\lVert E_T\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2/2. ◻

Why this handles momentum.

The theorem permits any AtA_t satisfying the two constraints. Hence it covers exact rank-kk Fanion updates from any heavy-ball or approximate Nesterov matrix, noisy gradients, adversarial singular-vector choices, and arbitrary learning-rate schedules. Momentum can change the selected singular directions; it does not increase the rank or nuclear norm of a single rank-kk partial isometry.

Corollary 2 (Necessary iterations at a constant learning rate). If f(XT)≤ϵf(X_T)\leq\epsilon and γt=γ>0\gamma_t=\gamma>0, then

T≥[‖E0‖*−2rϵ]+Lkγ,∑j>kTsj2≤2ϵ.\label{eq:necessary-T} T\geq \frac{[\lVert E_0\rVert_*-\sqrt{2r\epsilon}]_+}{\sqrt L\,k\gamma}, \qquad \sum_{j>kT}s_j^2\leq2\epsilon.(6)
More generally, the first inequality bounds ∑tγt\sum_t\gamma_t.

A tight separation after arbitrary step-size tuning

For f(X)=‖X−Ir‖F2/2f(X)=\lVert X-I_r\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2/2, X0=0X_0=0, every rank-kk update method satisfies f(XT)f(X0)≥max⁡{1−kT/r,0}.\frac{f(X_T)}{f(X_0)}\geq\max\{1-kT/r,0\}. Fanion-kk with exact line search and a diagonal tie choice attains this bound: it fixes kk previously uncorrected coordinates in each step (fewer in the last step). Thus it needs exactly ⌈r/k⌉\lceil r/k\rceil steps to reach zero, whereas Muon, F-Muon, and S-Muon reach zero in one step because their directions are proportional to IrI_r on the first iteration. For arbitrary positive spectra or tie choices this particular attainment claim need not hold; the lower bound still does.

What hybrids change

For H≠0H\ne0, the F-Fanion singular vectors are shared by its two summands, so ‖DF,k(H)‖*=ak+b‖H‖*‖H‖F≤ak+br\lVert D_{F,k}(H)\rVert_*=ak+b\frac{\lVert H\rVert_*}{\lVert H\rVert_{\mathrm F}}\leq ak+b\sqrt r when rank⁡H≥k\operatorname{rank}H\geq k. The S-Fanion satisfies ‖DS,k(H)‖*≤ak+bηrmn.\lVert D_{S,k}(H)\rVert_*\leq ak+b\eta\sqrt{r\,mn}. Consequently (5) holds for these methods with kk in the step budget replaced by these respective upper bounds. Their updates can have full rank, so (4) with the original kk is no longer available.

For r=500r=500, a=b=1/2a=b=1/2, the F-Neon capacity upper bound is about 11.68, versus 1 for Neon and 500 for Muon. At γF=0.015\gamma_F=0.015, the same new draw requires at least 2,604 F-Neon steps by this bound, versus 117 for F-Muon. The rank floor disappears, but a large nuclear-budget difference remains. The S bound is deliberately conservative because the nuclear norm of a sign matrix depends on its orientation and sign pattern.

Scope with normalization and decay.

The additive-iterate hypothesis holds for the synthetic runs, where code/L_smooth_tests/run_benchmarks_new.py sets norm_weight=False. If Xt+1=ctXt−γtAtX_{t+1}=c_tX_t-\gamma_tA_t, then XT=(∏tct)X0X_T=(\prod_t c_t)X_0 plus a low-rank sum with rescaled coefficients. Upper bounds retaining the gradient spectrum

Assume Frobenius LL-smoothness: f(X+Y)≤f(X)+⟨G,Y⟩+L2‖Y‖F2.f(X+Y)\leq f(X)+\langle G,Y\rangle+\frac L2\lVert Y\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2. For a nonzero descent direction DD, define

q(G,D)=⟨G,D⟩2‖G‖F2‖D‖F2∈[0,1].\label{eq:angle} q(G,D)=\frac{\langle G,D\rangle^2}{\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2\lVert D\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2}\in[0,1].(7)
The score is unchanged by positive scalar scaling of DD. For an exact Fanion with rank⁡G≥k\operatorname{rank}G\geq k, it is
qk(G)=Sk(G)2k‖G‖F2.\label{eq:qk} q_k(G)=\frac{S_k(G)^2}{k\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2}.(8)
For rank-deficient gradients, the same expression remains a conservative quantity; the exact cosine uses min⁡(k,rank⁡G)\min(k,\operatorname{rank}G) in the denominator.

Theorem 3 (Spectrum-dependent descent and PL convergence). Use the true gradient and set γt=Sk(Gt)/(Lk)\gamma_t=S_k(G_t)/(Lk). Then

f(Xt+1)≤f(Xt)−Sk(Gt)22Lk=f(Xt)−qk(Gt)2L‖Gt‖F2.\label{eq:spectrum-descent} f(X_{t+1})\leq f(X_t)-\frac{S_k(G_t)^2}{2Lk} =f(X_t)-\frac{q_k(G_t)}{2L}\lVert G_t\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2.(9)
In particular,
∑t<Tqk(Gt)‖Gt‖F2≤2L(f(X0)−f*).\label{eq:trajectory} \sum_{t<T}q_k(G_t)\lVert G_t\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2\leq2L(f(X_0)-f_*).(10)
If qk(Gt)≥q_k>0q_k(G_t)\geq\underline q_k>0 along the trajectory, then min⁡t<T‖Gt‖F2≤2L(f(X0)−f*)/(Tq_k)\min_{t<T}\lVert G_t\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2\leq2L(f(X_0)-f_*)/(T\underline q_k). If additionally ‖∇f(X)‖F2≥2μ(f(X)−f*)\lVert \nabla f(X)\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2\geq2\mu(f(X)-f_*) along the trajectory, then
f(XT)−f*≤(1−μq_k/L)T(f(X0)−f*).\label{eq:pl} f(X_T)-f_*\leq (1-\mu\underline q_k/L)^T(f(X_0)-f_*).(11)

Proof. Apply smoothness with Y=−γtDk(Gt)Y=-\gamma_tD_k(G_t) and use (1); if the active rank is smaller, ‖D‖F2≤k\lVert D\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2\leq k still gives the stated inequality. Substitute the step size, telescope, and then apply the respective lower bounds on qkq_k and ‖Gt‖F2\lVert G_t\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2. ◻

For a flat nonzero singular spectrum with rr modes, qk=k/rq_k=k/r. Thus the upper bounds retain a factor r/kr/k in deterministic iterations, and the PL rate is O((L/μ)(r/k)log⁡(Δ/ϵ))O((L/\mu)(r/k)\log(\Delta/\epsilon)) if this flatness condition persists. A more robust sufficient assumption is σ1/σr≤χ\sigma_1/\sigma_r\leq\chi, which gives qk≥k/(χ2r)q_k\geq k/(\chi^2r). This is a structural assumption to measure, not a consequence of smoothness.

At a common matrix GG, the exact Muon–Neon ratio is

qr(G)q1(G)=1r(‖G‖*‖G‖op)2.\label{eq:muon-neon} \frac{q_r(G)}{q_1(G)} =\frac1r\left(\frac{\lVert G\rVert_*}{\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}\right)^2.(12)
The ratio ranges from 1/r1/r to rr. Muon’s Frobenius decrease certificate is larger precisely when ‖G‖*/‖G‖op>r\lVert G\rVert_*/\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}>\sqrt r.

Why a worst-case bound cannot rank them.

In fact, inf⁡G≠0Sk(G)2k‖G‖F2=min⁡{1/k,k/r}.\inf_{G\ne0}\frac{S_k(G)^2}{k\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2} =\min\{1/k,k/r\}. To see this, express a decreasing nonnegative singular vector as a nonnegative sum of the step vectors vj=(1,…,1,0,…,0)v_j=(1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0). After imposing Sk=1S_k=1, it is a convex combination of vj/min⁡(k,j)v_j/\min(k,j). Convexity of the squared Euclidean norm gives its maximum as max⁡jj/min⁡(k,j)2=max⁡(1,r/k2)\max_j j/\min(k,j)^2=\max(1,r/k^2). Rank-one and flat vectors attain the endpoints. In particular, the worst-case constants for both Neon and Muon are 1/r1/r. Additional spectrum or curvature information is mathematically necessary.

Upper certificates are not observed speedups.

Equation (12) compares two certified decreases at the same point with separately optimized scalar steps. It does not bound the ratio of their complete training times. Different algorithms visit different points; a ratio of upper complexity bounds is not a lower bound on their actual runtime ratio.

F-Muon and S-Muon: explicit robustness bounds

Take H=G≠0H=G\ne0 and rank⁡G≥k\operatorname{rank}G\geq k, and put x=Sk(G)/‖G‖Fx=S_k(G)/\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm F}, so that x2/k=qkx^2/k=q_k.

Proposition 4 (Exact F-hybrid formula). For a,b≥0a,b\geq0, not both zero,

qF,k=(ax+b)2a2k+b2+2abx≥qk.\label{eq:fq} q_{F,k}= \frac{(ax+b)^2}{a^2k+b^2+2abx}\geq q_k.(13)

Proof. The numerator follows from ⟨G,DF,k⟩=aSk+b‖G‖F\langle G,D_{F,k}\rangle=aS_k+b\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm F}. The denominator follows from ⟨Dk,G/‖G‖F⟩=x\langle D_k,G/\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm F}\rangle=x. After multiplying by the positive denominators, the difference from qkq_k has numerator (k−x2)(2abx+b2)≥0(k-x^2)(2abx+b^2)\geq0. ◻

Thus adding a positive normalized true gradient can only improve the Frobenius angle. This explains why F-Muon can improve upon Muon on the synthetic task in this particular upper bound. It also proves that this bound alone cannot explain a universal advantage of Muon over NSGD or F-Neon: NSGD has angle one.

Proposition 5 (S-hybrid retention). For a>0a>0, b≥0b\geq0, η≥0\eta\geq0, and d0=#{Gij≠0}d_0=\#\{G_{ij}\ne0\},

q(G,DS,k)≥qk(G)(1+ρS,k)2,ρS,k=bηd0ak.\label{eq:sq} q(G,D_{S,k})\geq \frac{q_k(G)}{(1+\rho_{S,k})^2},\qquad \rho_{S,k}=\frac{b\eta\sqrt{d_0}}{a\sqrt k}.(14)

Proof. ⟨G,DS,k⟩=aSk+bη‖G‖1≥aSk\langle G,D_{S,k}\rangle=aS_k+b\eta\lVert G\rVert_1\geq aS_k, while ‖DS,k‖F≤ak+bηd0\lVert D_{S,k}\rVert_{\mathrm F}\leq a\sqrt k+b\eta\sqrt{d_0}. Substitute in (7). No sign assumption on the cross term ⟨Dk,sign⁡(G)⟩\langle D_k,\operatorname{sign}(G)\rangle is needed. ◻

For F-Muon with a=b=1/2a=b=1/2, the normalized-gradient component has Frobenius length only 1/r1/\sqrt r of the Muon component. For full-rank S-Muon on a dense matrix, the corresponding relative length is ηmax⁡(m,n)\eta\sqrt{\max(m,n)}. Thus the raw coefficient α=1/2\alpha=1/2 does not mean equal contribution to the actual update length. For the saved CIFAR shapes and η=0.003\eta=0.003, (14) preserves between 76.4% and 91.7% of the Muon angle certificate. These are uniform worst-case retentions, not predicted validation accuracies.

More generally, for a PSD curvature majorant ℋ\mathcal H and D=aD0+bD1D=aD_0+bD_1 with ⟨G,D1⟩≥0\langle G,D_1\rangle\geq0, the curvature score in the next section retains at least (1+ρ)−2(1+\rho)^{-2} of that of D0D_0, where ρ=b‖ℋ1/2D1‖F/(a‖ℋ1/2D0‖F)\rho=b\lVert \mathcal H^{1/2}D_1\rVert_{\mathrm F}/(a\lVert \mathcal H^{1/2}D_0\rVert_{\mathrm F}). Curvature: the comparison that can account for reversals

Suppose a quadratic upper model is valid along the considered step:

f(X−γD)≤f(X)−γA+γ22C(D),A=⟨G,D⟩,C(D)>0.\label{eq:curv-upper} f(X-\gamma D)\leq f(X)-\gamma A+\frac{\gamma^2}{2}C(D),\qquad A=\langle G,D\rangle,\quad C(D)>0.(15)
For example, C(D)=⟨D,ℋD⟩C(D)=\langle D,\mathcal HD\rangle if ∇2f≼ℋ\nabla^2 f\preceq\mathcal H on the segment. The optimized decrease certificate is
12ℐ(G,D),ℐ(G,D)=[A]+2C(D),γ=[A]+C(D).\label{eq:curv-score} \frac12\mathcal I(G,D),\qquad \mathcal I(G,D)=\frac{[A]_+^2}{C(D)},\quad \gamma=\frac{[A]_+}{C(D)}.(16)
If the model is only valid up to a maximal step, truncate γ\gamma and use (15) directly. For a quadratic, the model is exact at every step, so this is the actual optimal one-step decrease.

For (3),

C(D)=⟨D,MDN⟩=‖M1/2DN1/2‖F2.\label{eq:quad-curv} C(D)=\langle D,MDN\rangle=\lVert M^{1/2}DN^{1/2}\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2.(17)
Writing ui,viu_i,v_i for the singular vectors of GG, the Fanion curvature is C(Dk)=∑i,j≤k(ui⊤Muj)(vi⊤Nvj).C(D_k)=\sum_{i,j\leq k} (u_i^\top M u_j)(v_i^\top N v_j). The cross terms matter. Replacing this by LkLk may obscure the correct ordering. In particular, at a common point Muon’s curvature score beats Neon’s exactly when
(‖G‖*‖G‖op)2>C(Dr)C(D1).\label{eq:curvature-condition} \left(\frac{\lVert G\rVert_*}{\lVert G\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}\right)^2> \frac{C(D_r)}{C(D_1)}.(18)
This is a directly testable condition. Under the Frobenius majorant it reduces to the threshold in (12).

An explicit structured quadratic with the desired ordering

Take m=n=rm=n=r, N=IN=I, M=Λ=diag⁡(λi)≻0M=\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)\succ0, and X0=IX_0=I. The optimum of f(X)=⟨X,ΛX⟩/2f(X)=\langle X,\Lambda X\rangle/2 is zero, and G0=ΛG_0=\Lambda. Muon’s direction is II, so step one solves the problem. Neon with exact line search only removes the diagonal entry with the largest remaining λi\lambda_i, and requires exactly rr steps for zero loss. Fanion-kk takes ⌈r/k⌉\lceil r/k\rceil. S-Muon is proportional to II on this iterate, and likewise solves the problem in one step.

For F-Muon, choose step 1/a1/a rather than even optimizing it. Hence, for bounded κ\kappa, F-Muon is close to the one-step optimum as rr increases. This is a member of the manuscript’s quadratic class with a structured initialization, not a claim about all Gaussian initializations.

A counterexample to a universal Muon advantage

For f(X)=‖X‖F2/2f(X)=\lVert X\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2/2 at X=diag⁡(1,δ,…,δ)X=\operatorname{diag}(1,\delta,\ldots,\delta) with δ>0\delta>0, both directions have full-rank gradient inputs, and exact line search gives Muon one-step decreaseNeon one-step decrease=(1+(r−1)δ)2r.\frac{\text{Muon one-step decrease}}{\text{Neon one-step decrease}} =\frac{(1+(r-1)\delta)^2}{r}. A theorem imposing it in all cases would be false.

Stochastic gradients, momentum, and inexact LMOs

The next result keeps the estimator error visible instead of assuming orthogonalization of a stochastic matrix is unbiased.

Theorem 6 (Oracle-input error). Let H=G+EH=G+E, and let D̃\widetilde D satisfy ‖D̃‖op≤1,‖D̃‖*≤k,δ=Sk(H)−⟨H,D̃⟩≥0.\lVert \widetilde D\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq1,\quad\lVert \widetilde D\rVert_*\leq k,\qquad \delta=S_k(H)-\langle H,\widetilde D\rangle\geq0. Then

⟨G,D̃⟩≥Sk(G)−2Sk(E)−δ,‖D̃‖F2≤k.\label{eq:input-error} \langle G,\widetilde D\rangle\geq S_k(G)-2S_k(E)-\delta, \qquad \lVert \widetilde D\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2\leq k.(19)
Thus an LL-smooth objective obeys
f(X−γD̃)≤f(X)−γSk(G)+2γSk(E)+γδ+Lγ2k/2.\label{eq:noisy-decrease} f(X-\gamma\widetilde D)\leq f(X) -\gamma S_k(G)+2\gamma S_k(E)+\gamma\delta+L\gamma^2k/2.(20)

Proof. The support function of {D:‖D‖op≤1,‖D‖*≤k}\{D:\lVert D\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq1,\lVert D\rVert_*\leq k\} is SkS_k. Thus ⟨G,D̃⟩≥Sk(H)−δ−Sk(E)\langle G,\widetilde D\rangle\geq S_k(H)-\delta-S_k(E). Use the triangle inequality Sk(H)≥Sk(G)−Sk(E)S_k(H)\geq S_k(G)-S_k(E). Also ‖D̃‖F2≤‖D̃‖op‖D̃‖*≤k\lVert \widetilde D\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2\leq\lVert \widetilde D\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\lVert \widetilde D\rVert_*\leq k. Apply smoothness for the last assertion. ◻

For an arbitrary momentum matrix, take HH to be any positive rescaling of the actual oracle input; E=H−GE=H-G then contains both noise and tracking bias. For an approximate Newton–Schulz update outside the feasible set, the feasibility and ‖D̃‖F2≤k\lVert \widetilde D\rVert_{\mathrm F}^2\leq k claims cannot be assumed: use the actual direction and its norm in (15), or control its violation separately.

An explicit sample bound with minibatches

Use the normalized Frobenius step Xt+1=Xt−hDk(Ht)/kX_{t+1}=X_t-hD_k(H_t)/\sqrt k. Suppose qk(Gt)≥q_>0q_k(G_t)\geq\underline q>0 almost surely and 𝔼Sk(Ht−Gt)/k≤νk/B,\mathbb ES_k(H_t-G_t)/\sqrt k\leq\nu_k/\sqrt B, with exact LMOs. Writing Δ=f(X0)−f*\Delta=f(X_0)-f_*, telescoping (20) yields

For h=2Δ/(LT)h=\sqrt{2\Delta/(LT)}, it suffices to choose T≥8LΔq_ϵ2,B≥16νk2q_ϵ2T\geq\frac{8L\Delta}{\underline q\epsilon^2},\qquad B\geq\frac{16\nu_k^2}{\underline q\epsilon^2} to make the left side at most ϵ\epsilon. A fixed-batch momentum bound with the same spectral factor

For clarity, this calculation is for EMA heavy ball, not the implemented Nesterov variant. Let m−1=0m_{-1}=0, α=1−β∈(0,1]\alpha=1-\beta\in(0,1], mt=βmt−1+αgtm_t=\beta m_{t-1}+\alpha g_t, and Xt+1=Xt−hDk(mt)/kX_{t+1}=X_t-hD_k(m_t)/\sqrt k. 1T∑t<T𝔼‖Gt‖F≤1q_(ΔhT+Lh2+2β‖G0‖FαT+2βLhα+2σα2−α).\label{eq:ema-bound} \begin{split} \frac1T\sum_{t<T}\mathbb E\lVert G_t\rVert_{\mathrm F}\leq\frac1{\sqrt{\underline q}} \bigg(&\frac{\Delta}{hT}+\frac{Lh}{2} +\frac{2\beta\lVert G_0\rVert_{\mathrm F}}{\alpha T} +\frac{2\beta Lh}{\alpha} +2\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}}\bigg). \end{split}(22)
To verify this, unroll mt−Gt=−βt+1G0+∑s=1tβt−s+1(Gs−1−Gs)+α∑s=0tβt−sξs.m_t-G_t=-\beta^{t+1}G_0+ \sum_{s=1}^t\beta^{t-s+1}(G_{s-1}-G_s) +\alpha\sum_{s=0}^t\beta^{t-s}\xi_s. The drift norm is at most βLh/α\beta Lh/\alpha because each iterate moves by at most hh. Martingale orthogonality bounds the last term’s expected norm by σα/(2−α)\sigma\sqrt{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}. This displays the finite-precision terms as well as the fourth-power asymptotic term. It is a straightforward specialization of standard tracking arguments, not a claim to improve the universal stochastic first-order exponent.

Positive finding.

The median Muon–Neon ratio is between 1.67 and 3.81 across the six layers, and favors Muon in 88.0–98.4% of snapshots. Hence the observed spectra are broadly compatible with a better Muon descent certificate.

Negative finding that limits the conclusion.

The F-Neon–Muon surrogate ratio has median 1.13–2.08, favoring F-Neon. Yet F-Neon is empirically weaker. Therefore these spectra and a common Frobenius smoothness constant do not suffice to explain the complete ranking. Curvature, actual momentum alignment, and finite learning-rate budgets must be retained. F-Neon also has a much smaller update length at the manuscript’s inherited learning rate than an independently rescaled angle comparison permits.

Data limitations.

The CSVs store raw minibatch gradient norms, not the full population gradient, the momentum input, exact singular vectors, Hessian products, or update matrices. A nonlinear map of a stochastic gradient is not generally an unbiased map of the true gradient. Moreover, the reference Muon file has only 8 finite loss entries out of 250, although all the norm entries used here are finite. We do not fit loss rates to that column, infer the cause of its nonfinite entries, or use this cohort’s final accuracy as the manuscript’s eight-epoch result. Source filenames, hashes, counts, and finite-loss counts are saved in results/summary.json.

How to turn the conditional part into an experimental explanation

The missing measurements are specific and considerably smaller than a full Hessian. On common saved checkpoints and fixed evaluation batches:

  1. Save the actual oracle input HtH_t and the actual update matrix for each method, including Newton–Schulz approximation and scaling. Use a shared large-batch evaluation gradient GtG_t to measure ⟨Gt,Dt⟩\langle G_t,D_t\rangle and the error Ht−GtH_t-G_t after an appropriate positive rescaling of HtH_t.

  2. Measure ⟨Dt,∇2f(Xt)Dt⟩\langle D_t,\nabla^2 f(X_t)D_t\rangle with one Hessian–vector product per candidate direction, or use central loss differences at several small steps. On a nonconvex neural network a pointwise Hessian value is only a local estimate; verify the quadratic upper model at the actual step, rather than calling that value a global smoothness constant.

  3. Compare γt⟨Gt,Dt⟩−γt2Ct(Dt)/2\gamma_t\langle G_t,D_t\rangle-\gamma_t^2 C_t(D_t)/2 at the actual learning rate and ℐ(Gt,Dt)\mathcal I(G_t,D_t) at a separately optimized step. This separates a finite step-budget limitation from a directional curvature limitation. With multiple layers, include cross-layer curvature or justify a block-diagonal upper model before adding per-layer certificates.

  4. For the quadratic, compute E0E_0 from the original saved M,N,X0M,N,X_0 and substitute its singular values into Theorem 1. This yields a bound on the original run without a new optimizer sweep.

analyze.py --snapshot example.npz already computes the exact projections and, when the NPZ contains left and right, the exact quadratic curvature scores. It accepts oracle_input separately from gradient; therefore it does not silently equate the raw gradient with a Nesterov direction.

Implementation and manuscript details discovered during the check

These observations are recorded here; no original source was edited.

  1. Equation eq:ckfk_norm in the manuscript puts (1−α)/η(1-\alpha)/\eta before the entrywise ℓ1\ell_1 norm, while its update uses (1−α)ηsign⁡(H)(1-\alpha)\eta\operatorname{sign}(H). The support function of the actual Minkowski-sum ball is αSk(H)+(1−α)η‖H‖1\alpha S_k(H)+(1-\alpha)\eta\lVert H\rVert_1. Thus that equation’s coefficient should be (1−α)η(1-\alpha)\eta to match the displayed update.

  2. The manuscript defines a normalized EMA buffer Bt=βBt−1+(1−β)gtB_t=\beta B_{t-1}+(1-\beta)g_t and approximate Nesterov input gt+βBtg_t+\beta B_t. code/optimizers.py accumulates bt=βbt−1+gtb_t=\beta b_{t-1}+g_t and uses gt+βbtg_t+\beta b_t. With Bt=(1−β)btB_t=(1-\beta)b_t, the code input is gt+βBt/(1−β)g_t+\beta B_t/(1-\beta). The two mixtures are generally not positive scalar multiples, unlike the heavy-ball case. Theory should specify which one is being analyzed.

  3. The Frobenius smoothness constant of the quadratic is L=‖M‖op‖N‖opL=\lVert M\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\lVert N\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}. Some helper expressions in code/L_smooth_tests/problems.py use other, looser quantities. The obstruction proved here uses the exact Frobenius constant and does not rely on those helper expressions.

Answer to the research question.

Yes, there are substantially more discriminating bounds than a common O(ϵ−4)O(\epsilon^{-4}) statement: a rigorous finite-step explanation of Neon’s slow progress on the synthetic class, a tight r/kr/k separation example, and spectrum/curvature/noise-dependent upper bounds with explicit hybrid robustness. The quadratic obstruction is ready to use after substituting the original instance’s spectrum. A complete quantitative explanation of the CIFAR/LLM ordering remains open in this attempt because the required curvature and momentum measurements are absent. The existing log audit shows exactly where a simpler explanation succeeds and where it fails.
